The Disability Claim Starter

Michelle became the patient in 2017 and navigated the disability system from the other side, then trained as a Certified Return to Work Coordinator through NIDMAR. This is what she wishes someone had handed her in week one.

Back to the free tools
This is general education about how disability claims are usually organized. It is not legal advice, and it is not specific to your policy. Your own plan documents and your own providers are the authority on your claim.

1. Know which three documents make up your claim

Most short and long term disability claims are built from three separate forms, written by three different people, and they have to tell the same story.

  • Your statement. What you can and cannot do, in your own words. This is the one you control completely.
  • Your doctor's statement. The medical support for the restrictions. Your doctor writes this, but what they write depends heavily on what you have told them and what is in the chart.
  • Your employer's statement. Your job duties, hours and any accommodation history. This is where a mismatch with your own description often shows up.

Ask your plan or human resources contact for all three forms and the deadline for each on day one, not the week they are due.

2. Describe function, not feelings

Insurers assess what you can and cannot do. Pain and fatigue matter, but they matter through their effect on function. Statements that describe capacity are far easier to assess than statements that describe suffering.

  • Instead of my back is terrible, try I can sit for about fifteen minutes before I have to stand, and I cannot lift a full kettle.
  • Instead of I am exhausted, try I sleep about four hours, and by early afternoon I cannot follow a conversation or drive safely.
  • Instead of I cannot work, try my job needs six hours at a screen and lifting to twenty kilograms, and right now I manage twenty minutes at a screen and no lifting.

Name the actual duties of your actual job. A claim is assessed against those duties, not against work in general.

3. Keep a log from today

The single most useful thing you can do in week one is start a log, because a claim that is decided months later is decided on the record that exists. Five lines a day is enough. Keep it consistent rather than complete.

  • Symptoms and severity that day, in plain language.
  • What you could and could not do. Specific tasks, with rough durations.
  • Every contact. Date, who you spoke to, what was said, what they promised, and by when.
  • Every appointment, test and treatment, including ones you are waiting for.

4. Prepare your doctor's appointment before you go

Your doctor is filling in a form about your daily function, which is something they only see for ten minutes at a time. Bring the log. Bring your actual job duties in writing. Bring the specific form and the deadline. This is the appointment where preparation changes the outcome most.

5. If you are denied

A denial is not automatically the end. There is usually an internal appeal process with its own deadline, and that deadline is the thing to find first.

  • Get the reason in writing, and the specific policy definition they applied.
  • Find the appeal deadline and diarize it immediately.
  • Request your claim file. You are generally entitled to see what was used to decide.
  • Identify what was missing, then get that specific evidence rather than resending the same package.
  • Get advice if the amount or duration is significant. Advocacy support, and in some situations legal advice, is worth it early rather than late.
